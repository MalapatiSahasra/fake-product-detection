Reading these numbers: when the cascade says "genuine," it's right about 70% of the time — but it only recognizes 28% of genuine photos. Classical features (ORB keypoints, HSV histograms) can pin down one specific product against one reference; across hundreds of different products spanning five brands and photo styles, most genuine photos simply don't resemble any of the 20 sampled gallery references closely enough. That's a real limitation of training-free matching on high-diversity data, not a bug. Recall rises as the gallery grows (F1 0.39 with 20 references, 0.58 with 68). It works best on controlled photos: a fixed background and consistent lighting. In our tests the color check mostly rewards white studio backgrounds, and low light or a color cast breaks it, so treat a verdict as a screening aid, not proof. See Method below.

How the cascade works

Four independent checks vote; a photo passes if at most one disagrees (with no barcode in the photo: at least 2 of 3).

1

Logo · ORB + Brute-Force Hamming matching

Detects keypoints in the test photo and the best-matching image in a gallery of genuine references; counts good matches after a Lowe's ratio test.

2

Color · HSV histogram correlation

Compares 2D Hue–Saturation histograms (Value channel dropped for lighting invariance) against the best-matching gallery image.

3

Text · Tesseract OCR + string similarity

Reads printed text and compares it against a list of acceptable brand strings (NIKE, PUMA, ADIDAS, GUCCI, LV), catching misspellings like "NUMA" for "PUMA."

4

Barcode · format validation

Decodes any QR/barcode and checks it against an expected serial pattern. Only 1 of 1,565 raw photos in this dataset carried one, so it's excluded from the vote here.
